BGDNet: boundary-guided dual-backbone encoder–decoder network for

Abstract: Accurate segmentation of dermoscopic lesions is essential for computer-aided melanoma
assessment, but reliable delineation remains challenging because lesions often exhibit irregular shapes,
low contrast, and ambiguous boundaries. This study aimed to improve both lesion-region segmentation
and contour localization by better coordinating complementary local and global image representations.
We developed BGDNet, a boundary-guided dual-backbone encoder–decoder network that combines
TransXNet and Swin Transformer.
The model integrates local structural information with long-
range contextual information and uses boundary priors to guide feature interaction. Its performance
was evaluated on the ISIC-2016, ISIC-2018, and PH2 datasets and compared with representative
convolutional, Transformer-based, and hybrid segmentation methods using both region-based and
boundary-sensitive metrics. On ISIC-2018, BGDNet achieved an intersection over union (IoU) of
0.8813 ± 0.0014, a Dice similarity coeﬃcient (DSC) of 0.9330 ± 0.0009, a 95th percentile Hausdorﬀ
distance (HD95) of 5.84 ± 0.11 pixels, an average surface distance (ASD) of 1.34 ± 0.03 pixels, and
a boundary F-score at a 2-pixel tolerance (BF@2) of 0.868 ± 0.003. A recall (sensitivity) of 0.9602 ±
0.0021 and a speciﬁcity of 0.9847±0.0007 further characterize foreground–background behavior under
class imbalance. Additional in-dataset and cross-dataset experiments, together with component-wise
ablation and stage-wise boundary-prior visualization, provide complementary evidence for the model
design. These ﬁndings show that boundary-guided interaction between complementary encoders can
improve both lesion overlap and contour accuracy, particularly for lesions with irregular or weakly
deﬁned margins. BGDNet therefore provides a promising framework for accurate dermoscopic lesion
segmentation.
Keywords: dermoscopic image segmentation; skin lesion segmentation; boundary-aware
segmentation; dual-backbone network; transformer-based models

1. Introduction
The incidence of melanoma continues to increase globally, driven by environmental factors,
greater ultraviolet exposure, and population aging, with a rising incidence among younger
cohorts [1, 2]. Melanoma is highly aggressive, and early detection substantially improves survival
outcomes [3].
Early-stage lesions often present with subtle or atypical signs, which can lead to
misdiagnosis or diagnostic delay. Dermoscopy, a noninvasive imaging modality, plays a central role
in early screening and clinical assessment of cutaneous lesions [4, 5].
Nonetheless, dermoscopic
interpretation relies on clinician expertise and is subject to inter- and intra-observer variability;
manual delineation of lesion boundaries is time-consuming and inconsistent [6]. These limitations
motivate automated segmentation systems that are accurate, eﬃcient, and clinically reliable.
Deep learning has driven substantial progress in medical image segmentation.
Convolutional
neural networks (CNNs), notably U-Net and its variants, eﬀectively combine low-level spatial detail
with high-level semantic context through encoder–decoder structures and skip connections [7, 8].
However, convolutional operators are intrinsically local, which constrains the eﬀective receptive ﬁeld
and limits the modeling of long-range dependencies. This limitation is consequential for dermoscopic
images,
where lesions commonly exhibit irregular shapes,
weak contrast at margins,
and
heterogeneous textures.
Approaches such as dilated convolutions and multiscale fusion partially
alleviate this issue [9, 10], yet they do not provide a principled mechanism for capturing global
context.

that are critical for clinical interpretation. Hybrid CNN–Transformer architectures attempt to combine
convolutional spatial precision with attention-driven context [14,15], yet explicit boundary exploitation
and multiscale alignment across heterogeneous encoders remain challenging. Although boundary-
aware loss functions can complement region-based objectives, their eﬀectiveness depends on reliable

These characteristics substantially increase the diﬃculty of
accurate lesion delineation, particularly along lesion margins, and highlight the need for segmentation
frameworks that explicitly account for boundary information rather than relying solely on
region-based feature learning.
Despite recent progress,
aligning heterogeneous representations while preserving reliable
boundary information remains challenging. Direct concatenation or unconstrained attention between
local-structure and Transformer branches may introduce semantic and statistical mismatch,
particularly near low-contrast lesion margins. Furthermore, boundary information is frequently used
only as an auxiliary decoder output or a training objective rather than as a signal that directly
regulates cross-branch feature interaction.
To address these limitations, we introduce BGDNet, a boundary-guided dual-backbone network
for dermoscopic lesion segmentation. The model combines TransXNet [17] and Swin Transformer
to capture complementary local-detail and global-context representations. Rather than using Sobel
operators, attention, and deformable convolution as isolated components, BGDNet organizes them
within a boundary-prior-conditioned fusion framework. Feature-level boundary priors derived from
ﬁxed Sobel responses and a learnable projection directly regulate bidirectional interaction between the
two heterogeneous branches, making feature fusion more reliable around ambiguous lesion contours.
The principal contributions of this work are as follows:
(1) We construct a heterogeneous dual-backbone encoder using a four-stage Swin Transformer branch
and a TransXNet-XXS branch to provide complementary hierarchical representations. Because the
two branches produce features with diﬀerent channel characteristics and statistical distributions,
stage-speciﬁc 1×1 projections are applied before boundary extraction and cross-branch interaction,
providing compatible feature spaces for subsequent boundary-guided fusion.
(2) We introduce a boundary extraction module (BEM) that applies ﬁxed channel-wise Sobel operators
followed by a learnable projection to derive feature-level boundary priors. These priors provide
additional boundary guidance from intermediate representations and are not interpreted as pixel-
accurate clinical lesion boundaries.
(3) We introduce boundary-guided cross-attention (BGCA) to regulate bidirectional information
exchange between the two encoder branches. BEM-derived boundary priors are introduced into
the query and key pathways, whereas the value pathways retain the corresponding semantic
features. The boundary priors therefore inﬂuence cross-branch correspondence without replacing
the semantic content being transferred.

2.3. Hybrid segmentation methods
Hybrid architectures aim to unify the strengths of CNNs and Transformers by combining
convolutional locality with attention-based global reasoning.
Representative methods, including

In general, convolutional components provide precise spatial localization and texture cues,
whereas Transformer components contribute holistic context and long-range consistency. Fusion can
be performed at early, intermediate, or late stages and is frequently implemented through
cross-attention, gating, or feature-recalibration modules to adaptively exchange information between
branches.
Although hybrid methods have reported strong performance on International Skin Imaging
Collaboration (ISIC) benchmarks, several issues remain.
Many approaches prioritize region-level
overlap metrics and pixel-wise accuracy while under-specifying boundary modeling, which can yield
ambiguous or jagged contours in heterogeneous regions. In addition, heterogeneous encoders produce
features with diﬀerent resolutions, receptive ﬁelds, and statistical distributions. Naive concatenation
or additive fusion may blur edges or misalign ﬁne structures without explicit alignment.
Moreover, dual-pathway designs and deep fusion stacks often increase parameter counts and
inference latency, limiting applicability on resource-constrained platforms.
These observations
motivate boundary-aware alignment and multiscale fusion mechanisms that can eﬀectively translate
global context into clinically meaningful contour precision.

mechanisms. In contrast, BGDNet focuses speciﬁcally on using feature-derived boundary priors to

relying on general feature fusion alone.

3.7. Experimental settings and datasets
All experiments were implemented in PyTorch [39] and conducted on a single NVIDIA Tesla V100
graphics processing unit with 16 GB of memory. Images were resized to 352 × 352 and normalized
using the ImageNet channel means (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and standard deviations (0.229, 0.224, 0.225).
The corresponding lesion masks were resized to the same spatial resolution and converted to ﬂoating-
point tensors.
The models were trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 4. AdamW [40] was used with an
initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a weight decay of 1 × 10−4. Parameter gradients were clipped
element-wise to the interval [−0.5, 0.5]. The learning-rate decay factor was 0.1, and the decay epoch
was set to 200.
No random data augmentation was enabled in the principal experiments.
The training loader
shuﬄed the samples and discarded the ﬁnal incomplete batch. The Swin branch was initialized using
the Swin-Tiny ImageNet-22K pretrained checkpoint, whereas the TransXNet branch was initialized
from the corresponding pretrained TransXNet checkpoint. Newly introduced BEM, BGCA, decoder,
deformable-oﬀset, and prediction-head parameters were optimized jointly with the two encoders.
The reported optimization and loss hyperparameters were pre-speciﬁed and kept ﬁxed across the
reported BGDNet experiments. The 200 epochs specify the maximum optimization horizon rather
than the epoch automatically used for testing. Checkpoint selection was based on the mean validation
Dice similarity coeﬃcient (DSC) of the lesion-mask prediction. The checkpoint with the highest
mean validation DSC was retained for ﬁnal evaluation, and the held-out test sets did not participate in
hyperparameter tuning or checkpoint selection. Weight decay and gradient clipping were used as
regularization and optimization-stabilization measures.
During inference, the segmentation and boundary logits were bilinearly interpolated to the spatial
resolution of the corresponding ground-truth mask.
A sigmoid function was then applied, and a
threshold of 0.5 was used to obtain the binary lesion prediction. No morphological post-processing or
test-time augmentation was used.
Experiments were conducted on ISIC-2016 [41], ISIC-2018 [42], and PH2 [43]. For ISIC-2018,
the oﬃcial 1000-image test set was retained for ﬁnal evaluation, while the original training pool was

0.5.

The boundary-supervision target is generated using a normalized Sobel gradient magnitude. This
soft target avoids the need to select a ﬁxed morphological boundary width, but it remains dependent on
the quality and interpolation of the original segmentation annotation. Annotation uncertainty, gradual
lesion transitions, and strong artifact edges may therefore inﬂuence the auxiliary boundary signal.
The recall (sensitivity) and speciﬁcity metrics provide complementary information under
foreground–background imbalance. The reported recall indicates strong recovery of lesion pixels,
while the high speciﬁcity reﬂects eﬀective rejection of background pixels.

further emphasizes foreground overlap during optimization.
Cross-dataset evaluation provides a complementary assessment under dataset shift.

The deformable reconstruction pathway should be distinguished from the newly proposed
components. MSDC is adapted from MSDUNet [36] and is retained as the reconstruction pathway in
the component-wise comparisons; it is therefore not regarded as an independent methodological
contribution of BGDNet.
6. Limitations and future work
Several limitations remain. First, ﬁxed Sobel operators provide a simple ﬁrst-order boundary prior
but cannot adapt their local gradient operators to every lesion appearance. Deep-stage BEM responses
are also spatially coarse and may include non-lesion or artifact-related activations.
Second, the annotation-derived soft boundary target cannot explicitly represent inter-observer
uncertainty.
Multi-annotator labels, probabilistic contours, signed-distance representations, or
uncertainty-aware targets may provide more faithful supervision.
Third, bidirectional cross-attention and deformable reconstruction increase parameter count,
computation, and memory requirements. Eﬃcient or linear attention, lighter encoders, knowledge
distillation, structured pruning, and reduced deformable computation are therefore relevant future
directions.
Fourth, the cross-dataset experiment uses PH2 as the unseen target and therefore provides only a
limited assessment of dataset shift.
The three public datasets do not cover the full diversity of
acquisition devices, clinical centers, skin appearances, and annotation practices. External multi-center
validation with ﬁxed trained models remains necessary before making claims of clinical readiness.
Fifth, the quantitative comparisons with previously published methods include literature-reported
results rather than uniformly retrained implementations of all competing architectures. Diﬀerences in
dataset
partitioning,
preprocessing,
pretrained
initialization,
training
strategy,
and
metric
implementation among the source studies may therefore aﬀect direct numerical comparability. A fully
controlled benchmark in which all competing methods are retrained and evaluated under an identical
protocol would provide a stronger basis for future comparisons.

7. Conclusion
This work proposed BGDNet, a boundary-guided dual-backbone encoder–decoder network for
dermoscopic lesion segmentation.
The model combines a hierarchical Swin Transformer with
TransXNet and uses channel-wise Sobel feature responses to condition bidirectional cross-branch
attention. Deformable skip-feature alignment and multi-scale deformable aggregation are then used to
progressively reconstruct the lesion mask and auxiliary boundary map.
Experiments on ISIC-2016, ISIC-2018, and PH2 demonstrate strong in-dataset region-overlap and
boundary-localization performance for BGDNet. Cross-dataset experiments further show that ISIC-
trained models retain useful performance on the held-out PH2 test subset without target-domain ﬁne-
tuning.
The ablation results indicate that the beneﬁt of BGDNet does not arise only from introducing
boundary priors.
Using these priors to guide bidirectional interaction between the heterogeneous
encoders provides a further improvement over direct boundary-prior incorporation and conventional
bidirectional cross-attention.
The stage-wise visualization also supports interpreting deep BEM
responses as feature-level boundary priors rather than precise lesion contours.
Overall, the results support feature-derived boundary conditioning as an eﬀective strategy for
coordinating complementary local and contextual representations. Future work will investigate more
eﬃcient cross-attention, uncertainty-aware boundary supervision, stronger robustness to artifact
edges, lightweight dual-backbone variants, and external multi-center validation.
